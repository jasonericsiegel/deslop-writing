# Plain-Language Rules

*Rules version: 1.2*

The add half of plain language: rebuild the cut-down text into a clear, human voice.

## The Zinsser block (apply to every sentence)

```
1. Strip to cleanest form. Cut words that do no work.
2. Fight clutter. "Due to the fact that" -> "because." Kill filler.
3. Choose the plain word when a technical term and a plain term mean the
   same thing. "Utilize" -> "use." "Facilitate" -> "help."
4. Keep terms of art that ARE the concept. Test: would replacing lose
   information, or just decoration?
5. Concrete over abstract. Short words over long.
6. Meaning-preservation check: does the simpler word make the same claim?
   "Near peak" and "near-superintelligent" describe different things.
   Keep the specific one.
```

Rule 6 is the load-bearing guardrail. It is repeated as Critical Rule 1 in SKILL.md for a reason: simplification's main failure mode is flattening a real claim. When the shorter word changes the meaning, keep the longer one. Plainness never outranks accuracy.

## The wider canon (each as an edit)

From Zinsser's *On Writing Well*, Strunk & White's *Elements of Style*, Orwell's six rules, and the US Federal Plain Language Guidelines — the rules that agree and add a move:

- **Omit needless words** (Strunk & White, rule 13). Every word earns its place. Cut redundant pairs ("basic fundamentals", "close proximity").
- **Active voice by default.** Find *was [verb]ed by*; flip it. Passive is fine only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.
- **Lead with the main point**, then the detail. Orientation before answer.
- **Short sentences. One idea per paragraph.**
- **A precise verb beats a verb plus an adverb.** *Ran quickly* → *sprinted*. An adverb that repeats the verb is clutter.
- **Cut weak qualifiers** — *very, really, quite, rather* — unless they change the meaning.
- **Plain word over fancy:** *use* not *utilize*, *help* not *facilitate*, *because* not *due to the fact that*, *now* not *at this point in time*, *if* not *in the event that*.
- **Write like you talk** to a smart friend you respect: logical and thorough, trusting the reader to make obvious connections. Avoid breeziness (trying too hard to sound casual) and condescension (avoid opening with *actually*, *simply*, *just*).
- **Prefer contractions.** *We have* → *we've*, *is not* → *isn't*, *do not* → *don't*, *it is* → *it's*. Contractions are how people actually talk — the plainer, more human form. Expanding a contraction makes prose stiffer and more formal, the opposite of plain; it is a regression, never an improvement. Contractions are not an AI tell (see the catalog). The only exception is the rare case where the full form carries genuine emphasis (*do not* in a warning) — keep it then, by intent, not reflex.
- **Don't reach for the em-dash.** It's the single most common tell in current AI prose, and the rewrite pass is where it sneaks back in: rebuilding a sentence, the engine reaches for a dash by habit even after the subtract pass cleared the old ones. So this is an *add*-half rule, not just a tell to catch. When you rebuild, default to a period or comma between clauses, parentheses around an aside, a colon before a payoff. Keep an em-dash only where no other mark does the job — a true appositive or a sharp interruptive break — and at most one per paragraph. (This rule does not flatten meaning, so it never outranks rule 6; it changes punctuation, not claims.)

## Filler-to-plain quick swaps

| Wordy | Plain |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| at this point in time | now |
| in the event that | if |
| has the ability to | can |
| it is important to note that | (cut — just state it) |
| a large number of | many |
| in the process of -ing | (cut "in the process of") |

## The caveat that governs all of this

Run every rule mechanically at full strength and you get flat, monotonous prose — the opposite of a human voice, and the very thing this is meant to fix. Judgment outranks the checklist. As Orwell's last rule puts it: break any of these sooner than write something clumsy.
